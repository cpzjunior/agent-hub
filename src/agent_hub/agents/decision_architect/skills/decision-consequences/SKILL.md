# Decision Consequences

## Purpose

Understand the potential consequences of a decision and its alternatives.

The goal is to evaluate not only immediate and intended outcomes,
but also indirect effects, trade-offs, delayed impacts, and changes
to future decision possibilities.

Consequences should be considered in relation to the decision's
objectives, context, and uncertainties.

## When to use

Use this skill when:

- Alternatives need to be compared.
- Potential impacts are not sufficiently understood.
- A decision may create significant indirect effects.
- Trade-offs between objectives need clarification.
- Decisions may restrict future choices.
- Reversibility or long-term implications are relevant.
- New information changes previously expected consequences.

## Activities

### 1. Identify potential consequences

For each relevant alternative, identify potential outcomes.

Consider:

- Intended outcomes.
- Unintended outcomes.
- Direct consequences.
- Indirect consequences.
- Positive and negative impacts.
- Short-term and long-term effects.

Do not restrict the analysis to consequences explicitly mentioned
in the original proposal.

### 2. Identify affected parties and dimensions

Understand who or what may be affected.

Depending on the decision, consider:

- Customers and users.
- Employees and teams.
- Business units and organizations.
- Technology and infrastructure.
- Financial resources.
- Operations and processes.
- Security and compliance.
- Reputation and relationships.
- External stakeholders.

Consider different perspectives when evaluating the same consequence.

### 3. Analyze cause-and-effect relationships

Investigate how consequences may emerge from the decision.

Consider:

- Direct causal relationships.
- Secondary and indirect effects.
- Dependencies between consequences.
- Feedback loops.
- Delayed effects.
- Conditions that may amplify or reduce impacts.

Avoid treating every possible consequence as equally likely
or equally significant.

### 4. Evaluate trade-offs

Identify conflicts between objectives and potential outcomes.

Consider:

- Benefits versus costs.
- Short-term gains versus long-term effects.
- Performance versus flexibility.
- Efficiency versus resilience.
- Autonomy versus control.
- Speed versus quality.
- Local optimization versus broader organizational impact.

Make trade-offs explicit instead of assuming that one alternative
is superior in every dimension.

### 5. Explore scenarios

Consider plausible scenarios that may influence the consequences
of each alternative.

Evaluate:

- Expected conditions.
- Favorable conditions.
- Adverse conditions.
- Changes in critical assumptions.
- Relevant external events.

Use scenarios to explore uncertainty, not to predict the future
with unjustified certainty.

### 6. Evaluate reversibility and future options

Understand how the decision may affect future choices.

Consider:

- Whether the decision can be reversed.
- The cost and effort required to reverse it.
- Commitments that become difficult to change.
- Dependencies created by the decision.
- Lock-in and path dependency.
- Future options that may become unavailable.
- Flexibility and optionality preserved by each alternative.

A decision that appears beneficial in the short term may impose
significant restrictions on future choices.

### 7. Evaluate timing and the cost of waiting

Consider whether the timing of the decision changes its consequences.

Evaluate:

- Benefits of acting now.
- Benefits of waiting for additional information.
- Costs of delaying action.
- Risks of premature commitment.
- Opportunities that may disappear over time.
- Changes in the environment while the decision remains open.

Do not assume that postponing a decision is neutral.

### 8. Review expected consequences

Update the analysis when new information or circumstances emerge.

Reassess consequences when:

- Objectives change.
- New risks are identified.
- Assumptions are invalidated.
- Alternatives are modified.
- Dependencies change.
- Actual outcomes differ from expectations.

## Interaction guidelines

- Analyze consequences in relation to objectives.
- Consider both direct and indirect effects.
- Include positive and negative consequences.
- Avoid exhaustive speculation without decision value.
- Make important trade-offs explicit.
- Distinguish plausible scenarios from predictions.
- Consider different stakeholder perspectives.
- Give special attention to irreversible or path-dependent decisions.
- Adjust analysis depth to the decision's importance and complexity.
- Revisit previous assessments when new information emerges.

## Expected output

Produce an updated consequence representation containing,
when applicable:

- Potential direct consequences.
- Potential indirect consequences.
- Positive and negative impacts.
- Affected stakeholders and dimensions.
- Relevant cause-and-effect relationships.
- Trade-offs between objectives.
- Plausible scenarios.
- Reversibility and future constraints.
- Timing considerations.
- Unresolved questions and assumptions.

The output should be structured so it can be incorporated into
the `consequences` field of the Decision Model.

## Important principles

- Consequences extend beyond immediate and intended outcomes.
- Indirect effects may be as important as direct effects.
- Alternatives should be evaluated across relevant dimensions.
- Trade-offs are inherent in many important decisions.
- Decisions may constrain future choices through commitments
  and dependencies.
- Reversibility and optionality influence the cost of commitment.
- Waiting also has consequences.
- Consequence analysis must evolve as the decision is reassessed.
