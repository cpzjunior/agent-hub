# Decision Evolution

## Purpose

Understand how a decision evolves over time and determine whether
its assumptions, objectives, alternatives, and expected consequences
remain valid.

The goal is to maintain alignment between the decision and the
reality in which it operates.

A decision should not be treated as permanently valid simply because
it was previously made.

## When to use

Use this skill when:

- New evidence becomes available.
- Circumstances change.
- Actual outcomes differ from expectations.
- Assumptions are invalidated.
- Objectives or priorities change.
- A decision requires reassessment.
- Lessons from implementation or execution may affect future choices.

## Activities

### 1. Review the current decision

Understand the current state of the decision.

Consider:

- What was originally intended?
- What assumptions supported the decision?
- Which alternative was selected, if any?
- What consequences were expected?
- What constraints and dependencies remain relevant?
- What has changed since the previous assessment?

Use the available Decision Model and history to reconstruct
the rationale behind the current state.

### 2. Compare expectations with actual outcomes

Evaluate what happened in relation to what was expected.

Consider:

- Expected versus actual results.
- Progress toward objectives.
- Changes in relevant indicators.
- Unexpected consequences.
- New risks and opportunities.
- Stakeholder feedback.
- Deviations from assumptions.

Distinguish between an unfavorable outcome and evidence that
the original decision was inadequate.

Not every deviation means the decision itself should be changed.

### 3. Identify the nature of required changes

Distinguish between three types of intervention.

#### Operational adjustment

The decision remains valid, but execution or implementation
requires correction.

Examples:

- Adjusting an implementation plan.
- Changing operational parameters.
- Correcting an execution issue.
- Reallocating resources.

#### Decision revision

The original decision is reconsidered because its assumptions,
objectives, alternatives, or expected consequences have changed.

Examples:

- Revisiting the selected alternative.
- Changing priorities.
- Reassessing risks.
- Revising commitments.

#### New decision

The circumstances require a distinct decision rather than
a revision of the existing one.

Examples:

- A new initiative emerges.
- A previously independent concern becomes a separate decision.
- A new choice must be made as a consequence of the original decision.

Preserve relationships between related decisions when relevant.

### 4. Reassess the decision architecture

When significant changes occur, revisit the relevant dimensions:

- Domain: has the relevant reality or its boundaries changed?
- Context: have circumstances, stakeholders, or constraints changed?
- Objectives: are the desired outcomes still valid?
- Uncertainties: have assumptions or risks changed?
- Consequences: are expected impacts still plausible?
- Evolution: what has been learned from previous outcomes?

Do not assume that every dimension needs to be reassessed
after every change.

Focus on the dimensions affected by the new information.

### 5. Preserve decision rationale and history

Record relevant changes so that the evolution of the decision
remains understandable.

Preserve, when applicable:

- Previous assumptions.
- Previous objectives.
- Alternatives considered.
- Reasons for selecting or rejecting alternatives.
- Expected consequences.
- Significant changes in context.
- Evidence supporting revisions.
- Lessons learned.

Do not silently replace previous conclusions when their historical
context is relevant.

### 6. Establish review signals

Identify conditions that may justify reassessing the decision.

Examples include:

- An assumption becomes invalid.
- A critical risk materializes.
- An objective is no longer relevant.
- A significant constraint changes.
- Expected results are not achieved.
- A dependency changes.
- A previously unavailable alternative becomes viable.

Review signals should be relevant to the decision rather than
arbitrary calendar events.

### 7. Incorporate learning

Use experience and evidence to improve the understanding
of the current decision and future decisions.

Consider:

- What worked as expected?
- What did not work?
- Which assumptions were incorrect?
- Which consequences were underestimated?
- What information would have improved the original analysis?
- What should be considered in future decisions?

Learning should update the Decision Model when it changes
the current understanding.

## Interaction guidelines

- Do not assume that every change requires a new decision.
- Distinguish execution problems from decision problems.
- Preserve relevant historical information.
- Explain why a previous conclusion is being reconsidered.
- Use evidence rather than hindsight alone.
- Avoid treating the original decision as permanently binding.
- Do not revise objectives or assumptions silently.
- Consider dependencies and consequences of changing an existing decision.
- Keep the decision open when evidence is insufficient for closure.

## Expected output

Produce an updated evolution representation containing,
when applicable:

- Current decision status.
- Relevant changes since the previous assessment.
- Expected versus actual outcomes.
- Deviations and their explanations.
- Operational adjustments.
- Decision revisions.
- Related new decisions.
- Updated assumptions and evidence.
- Lessons learned.
- Conditions for future reassessment.

The output should update the Decision Model and generate
corresponding Decision Events.

## Important principles

- Decisions evolve as circumstances and knowledge change.
- A deviation does not automatically invalidate a decision.
- Operational adjustments and decision revisions are different.
- Historical rationale should remain understandable.
- New evidence may justify revisiting previous conclusions.
- Learning is part of decision evolution.
- Decision evolution is continuous when relevant, not a mandatory
  periodic process.
