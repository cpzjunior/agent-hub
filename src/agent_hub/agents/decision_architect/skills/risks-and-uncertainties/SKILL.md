# Risks and Uncertainties

## Purpose

Understand what is uncertain about a decision and how that uncertainty
may affect its outcomes.

The goal is not to eliminate all uncertainty, but to make relevant
uncertainties explicit, evaluate their implications, and determine
appropriate responses.

Uncertainty may represent both threats and opportunities.

## When to use

Use this skill when:

- Important assumptions have not been validated.
- Relevant information is incomplete or unreliable.
- Potential events may affect decision outcomes.
- The consequences of uncertainty are significant.
- New evidence changes the assessment of existing risks.
- The decision requires commitments under uncertain conditions.
- Opportunities may emerge from uncertain circumstances.

## Activities

### 1. Identify uncertainties

Identify what is not sufficiently known or predictable.

Consider:

- Unverified assumptions.
- Incomplete information.
- Unpredictable external events.
- Technical unknowns.
- Market or business uncertainty.
- Stakeholder behavior.
- Dependencies on other decisions or initiatives.
- Uncertain future conditions.

Distinguish uncertainty from established facts.

Do not assume that every unknown represents a risk.

### 2. Identify critical assumptions

Identify assumptions on which the decision depends.

For each relevant assumption, consider:

- What is being assumed?
- Why is the assumption necessary?
- What evidence supports it?
- How confident are we?
- What happens if it is incorrect?
- Can it be validated before making a commitment?

Prioritize assumptions whose failure could materially change
the decision or its outcomes.

### 3. Identify and characterize risks

A risk represents an uncertain event or condition that may affect
the objectives or outcomes of the decision.

For each relevant risk, consider:

- Its underlying cause.
- The uncertain event or condition.
- Its potential consequences.
- Likelihood of occurrence.
- Magnitude of impact.
- Exposure and duration.
- Detectability.
- Available response capabilities.

Consider both threats and opportunities.

Avoid confusing an existing problem with a future uncertain event.

### 4. Evaluate uncertainty and risk

Assess the significance of identified uncertainties and risks.

Consider:

- How much is known about the situation.
- How reliable the available evidence is.
- How likely the relevant event may be.
- How severe its potential consequences are.
- How early it could be detected.
- How much time would be available to respond.
- Whether the decision is reversible.

Use qualitative assessments when numerical estimates are not
supported by evidence.

Do not introduce false precision through arbitrary probabilities
or scores.

### 5. Define possible responses

Consider appropriate responses to identified risks.

Possible strategies include:

- Avoid: change the decision or approach to eliminate exposure.
- Reduce: decrease likelihood or impact.
- Transfer or share: distribute exposure through contractual,
  organizational, or other arrangements.
- Accept: consciously retain the exposure.

For opportunities, consider approaches that increase their likelihood
or potential benefit.

Select responses according to the nature of the risk, the objectives,
available resources, and the decision context.

### 6. Reduce uncertainty

Determine whether additional information or experimentation
could improve the decision.

Possible approaches include:

- Targeted research.
- Technical investigation.
- Prototypes.
- Proofs of concept.
- Pilot implementations.
- Stakeholder validation.
- Controlled experiments.
- Scenario analysis.

Evaluate the cost of acquiring information against its potential
value to the decision.

Consider progressive commitment when uncertainty is significant:
make smaller, reversible commitments while gathering evidence
before making larger commitments.

### 7. Establish monitoring and review

Identify signals that may indicate changes in relevant uncertainties
or risks.

Consider:

- Early warning indicators.
- Changes in assumptions.
- External events.
- New evidence.
- Risk exposure.
- Effectiveness of planned responses.
- Conditions that would justify reconsidering the decision.

Update assessments when circumstances change.

## Interaction guidelines

- Distinguish facts, hypotheses, assumptions, and unknowns.
- Do not present uncertain estimates as established facts.
- Focus on uncertainties that may materially affect the decision.
- Avoid generating exhaustive lists of hypothetical risks.
- Explain the relevance of significant risks to objectives.
- Do not assume that uncertainty must always be eliminated.
- Consider opportunities as well as threats.
- Recommend investigation when its expected value justifies
  its cost.
- Revisit previous assessments when new evidence emerges.

## Expected output

Produce an updated uncertainty and risk representation containing,
when applicable:

- Identified uncertainties.
- Critical assumptions.
- Supporting evidence and confidence.
- Relevant risks and their causes.
- Potential impacts.
- Qualitative likelihood and impact assessments.
- Proposed responses.
- Opportunities.
- Monitoring indicators.
- Unresolved questions.

The output should be structured so it can be incorporated into
the `risks` and `evidence` fields of the Decision Model.

## Important principles

- Uncertainty is not necessarily a threat.
- Risks arise from uncertain conditions or events that may affect
  decision outcomes.
- Assumptions should be explicit and open to validation.
- Risk assessment should be proportional to the decision's importance.
- Numerical precision must be supported by evidence.
- Information gathering and experimentation can reduce uncertainty.
- Decisions under uncertainty may benefit from progressive commitment.
- New evidence may require revisiting objectives, alternatives,
  and expected consequences.
