# Decision Domain

## Purpose

Build and maintain a useful representation of the domain in which a
decision exists.

The goal is to understand the relevant portion of reality affected by
the decision, not to describe the entire surrounding environment.

## When to use

Use this skill when:

- A new decision is being analyzed.
- The problem or its boundaries are unclear.
- Relevant elements or stakeholders have not been identified.
- New information changes the understanding of the domain.
- The current domain representation is insufficient to evaluate
  alternatives or consequences.

## Activities

### 1. Identify the domain

Understand the subject and the portion of reality involved in the decision.

Identify:

- What the decision concerns.
- Which problem or opportunity is being addressed.
- Which parts of the environment are relevant.
- What is explicitly outside the scope.

Do not assume that the initial description provided by the user
accurately represents the actual domain.

### 2. Establish boundaries

Identify the limits of the domain being analyzed.

Consider:

- Organizational boundaries.
- Technical boundaries.
- Business boundaries.
- Geographic boundaries.
- Temporal boundaries.
- Regulatory or contractual boundaries.

Distinguish between elements inside the domain and external elements
that influence it.

### 3. Identify relevant elements

Identify the elements that participate in or influence the domain.

Depending on the situation, these may include:

- People and stakeholders.
- Organizations and teams.
- Systems and applications.
- Processes and activities.
- Data and information.
- Resources and infrastructure.
- Products and services.
- External entities and regulations.

Only include elements relevant to the decision.

### 4. Identify characteristics and relationships

Understand how relevant elements interact.

Consider:

- Dependencies.
- Information flows.
- Responsibilities.
- Interactions.
- Constraints.
- Control and influence relationships.
- Relevant behaviors.

Avoid treating elements as isolated entities when their relationships
affect the decision.

### 5. Evaluate the level of abstraction

Choose a level of detail appropriate to the decision.

Avoid two extremes:

- An overly broad representation that does not help distinguish
  relevant alternatives.
- An excessively detailed representation that introduces unnecessary
  complexity.

The representation should be sufficient to support the current
decision analysis.

### 6. Review the domain representation

Check whether the current understanding is consistent with the
available information.

Identify:

- Missing elements.
- Unclear boundaries.
- Relevant external influences.
- Contradictions.
- Areas requiring further investigation.

Revise the representation when new information changes the
understanding of the domain.

## Interaction guidelines

- Do not turn this activity into a rigid questionnaire.
- Ask only questions that materially improve the domain representation.
- Prefer exploring one relevant uncertainty at a time.
- Make assumptions explicit instead of presenting them as facts.
- Preserve relevant information already established in the Decision Model.
- Revisit previous conclusions when new information contradicts them.
- Do not force the user to complete every domain element before
  continuing the decision analysis.

## Expected output

Produce an updated domain representation containing, when applicable:

- Domain description.
- Boundaries and scope.
- Relevant elements.
- Relevant relationships.
- External influences.
- Unresolved questions and assumptions.

The output should be structured so it can be incorporated into the
`domain` field of the Decision Model.

## Important principles

- The domain is a representation of reality, not reality itself.
- The representation must be proportional to the decision's complexity.
- Domain understanding can evolve throughout the decision process.
- Changes in the domain may require revisiting context, objectives,
  uncertainties, alternatives, and consequences.
- Domain analysis is not a mandatory first step that must be completed
  before other decision activities.
